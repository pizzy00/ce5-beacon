COULD WE EVEN SEE THEM?
People assume that if something were flying around up there, a big enough telescope would have photographed it by now. That turns out to be a much weaker argument than it sounds, and not for the reason most people give. It is not about the size of the mirror.
Something crossing the sky near the speed of light arrives almost with its own light: from a light-year out at 99% of c you get 3.7 days of warning, and at 99.9% under nine hours. It sweeps 45 degrees per second at the Moon's distance, so it crosses Hubble's whole field in about a thousandth of a second and leaves a faint streak rather than a picture. And the Doppler shift moves it out of visible light altogether: reflected light off something inbound at 99% of c is boosted around 199 times, so green light arrives as an X-ray. An optical telescope is simply blind to it. Radar is worse.
What you would actually catch is the wake, not the hull. Anything moving that fast through interstellar hydrogen is hitting protons at billions of electron-volts, dumping megawatts per square metre and throwing off gamma rays. It would look like a moving gamma-ray source with a bow shock, which is a job for Fermi or CTA, not for Hubble.
None of that is evidence that anything is out there. It only means the absence of a photograph is close to worthless as an argument. We are not looking with the right instruments, in the right band, on the right timescale.

WHAT OUR TELESCOPES CAN AND CANNOT DO ✕
REAL PHYSICS, NOT LORE. SOURCES AT THE BOTTOM. THE CONCLUSION IS NARROW ON PURPOSE.
01 · YOU CANNOT SEE IT COMING
At exactly the speed of light an object arrives together with its own light: zero warning. Just below it, the warning time is the distance divided by c, times (1/β − 1). From one light-year out that is 3.7 days at 0.99c, 8.8 hours at 0.999c, and 41 days at 0.9c. So the scenario where somebody spots it early and points a telescope at it does not exist. You only ever see it during or after the pass.
02 · IT CROSSES THE FIELD IN A MILLISECOND
Angular rate is c divided by distance. At the Moon's distance that is 45 degrees per second. Hubble's ACS has a 3 arcminute field, so the object is across it in 1.1 milliseconds, and each 0.05 arcsecond pixel gets about 310 nanoseconds of exposure.
Run the photons for a 100 m hull at 50% albedo at lunar distance, which would be about magnitude +10 if it held still: roughly 3.5 million photons per second into a 2.4 m mirror, times 310 ns, is about one photon per pixel. The trail covers some 3,600 pixels, so around 2,000 electrons total against read noise of about 240 electrons — a matched-filter signal-to-noise near 8. Detectable as a streak, if you already knew where to look and were filtering for tracks. That is Rubin's job, not Hubble's.
The counter-intuitive part: trailing loss turns the usual inverse-square law into an inverse-linear one. Farther away means slower angular motion means longer dwell per pixel. Distance hurts you half as fast as instinct says.
03 · THEN RELATIVITY TAKES IT AWAY
Reflected light off an inbound object gets a double Doppler boost: (1+β)/(1−β), which at 0.99c is 199 times. Green light at 500 nm arrives at 2.5 nm, around 500 electron-volts. Your optical telescope sees nothing; you wanted Chandra, XMM or NuSTAR. The reflection is also beamed into a cone about 8 degrees wide, so off-axis there is nothing at all. On the way out the same effect dims it by 11.5 magnitudes and drags it to about 7 micrometres — JWST's band, at hopeless flux.
Radar fails harder. A 10 GHz X-band ping returns near 2 terahertz. No receiver is listening there, and the target has left long before the round trip finishes.
04 · WHAT YOU WOULD ACTUALLY DETECT IS THE WAKE
At a Lorentz factor of 10 through one proton per cubic centimetre, the craft sees a proton flux around 3×1015 per square metre per second at 8.4 GeV each: roughly 4 megawatts per square metre of frontal area, so 400 MW across a 100 m² cross-section. 8.4 GeV is well over the pion threshold, and neutral pions decay to gamma rays.
So it is a moving GeV gamma-ray point source with a bow shock, beamed forward. Fermi-LAT and CTA territory. And any real relativistic drive is far brighter than the hull it is pushing — which is exactly the channel serious SETI proposals have argued for.
05 · GENUINELY FASTER THAN LIGHT WOULD LOOK STRANGE
Ballistic FTL: it would appear suddenly at closest approach and then split into two images running in opposite directions along its track, preceded by a light boom. Unmistakable. Nothing in the sky does this.
A warp bubble is locally subluminal, so no image doubling. Its collapse would ring a short, very high frequency gravitational-wave burst — above LIGO's band, needing detectors nobody has funded. And a bubble sweeps up interstellar particles and dumps them forward on arrival, so the arrival would read as a gamma-ray burst rather than a landing.
06 · WE ALREADY PHOTOGRAPH APPARENT FTL ROUTINELY
VLBI images show jet knots in M87 and 3C 279 moving at an apparent 5 to 20 times light speed. That is pure light-travel-time projection, a solved and frankly boring problem. Resolving apparent superluminal motion is not the hard part. The real thing is.
WHAT THIS DOES AND DOES NOT SHOW
It does not show that anything is out there. It shows that "no telescope has ever photographed one" is a far weaker argument than it is usually made to carry. Wrong instruments, wrong band, wrong timescale. Absence of evidence, from instruments that were never built to look for this, is close to no evidence at all.
One caveat stated plainly: section 04 assumes a compact solid craft with an ordinary drive. A craft decelerating on a magnetic sail would instead be a bright synchrotron source — arguably the loudest signature of all, because it is deliberately coupling to the interstellar medium.
